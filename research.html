theREALinst

Physically modeled instruments, played by virtual performers who have to learn them first.

In development since 2026 · C++, Apple Metal, Python · Dartmouth College

How does a virtual instrument change when it comes with a player who must learn to control it?

Most physical models are played by writing their control signals directly: a lip frequency curve for a trumpet, a hammer velocity for a piano note. Real instruments are not played that way. A trumpeter does not know the lip frequency of a G5; they know what it feels like to land it, and they learned that by practicing on one instrument with one body.

theREALinst pairs each instrument model with a simulated performer. The performer practices on that particular instrument, keeps what it learned, and then plays a score by listening and correcting itself. The same score on a different instrument, or with a different player, comes out differently for physical reasons rather than random ones. My longer aim is a tool that lets composers and performers explore techniques and instruments they cannot easily get their hands on, including speculative instruments assembled from modeled parts.

The project began as the audio engine of a game I am co-developing and has grown into the center of my research.

Piano: dampers

A damper is a piece of felt that rests on the string and stops it when the key is released. In one version of the model, at the moment the felt left the string or came back to it, the calculation gave the string a small push, as if it had been plucked lightly.

  • One C4 Played twice: first with that push, then with it fixed. With the push, the damper lifting makes a faint sound just before the hammer strikes, and a quiet tail hangs on after the key is released. The note itself is the same in both.
  • Pedal only, no keys No key is played; only the sustain pedal goes down and comes back up. The pedal lifts every damper at once, so every string in the piano gets that small push at the same moment.

The instruments

Each instrument is assembled from parts. Each part is a physical model taken from a published paper or built from published measurements. Where no measurement exists, the value is my estimate, and I say so below.

theREALbrass: trumpet, trombone, horn, tuba

  • Air column: a one-dimensional lossy horn equation, solved sample by sample by finite differences (Bilbao & Chick, 2013), with the wall losses fitted as in Harrison-Harsley (2018).
  • Bore shape: the trumpet's is fitted to the seven impedance peaks measured by Bilbao & Chick (2013), and lands within 1% of each. The trombone's is taken section by section from Braden's (2006) measurements of a real trombone. For the horn and tuba there are no published bore measurements, so I fitted their shapes to published dimensions and to their harmonic series.
  • Mouthpieces: manufacturers' published dimensions.
  • Valves and slide: each valve joins three tubes and opens gradually as the piston moves (Bilbao, 2011). The pistons have mass, springs and felt stops and can be half-pressed; these mechanical values are my estimates. The trombone slide changes the length of tube smoothly between positions.
  • Brassiness: at loud dynamics the wave steepens as it travels (Hirschberg et al., 1996; Myers et al., 2012). The trumpet and horn model this in the straight part of the tube; the trombone and tuba model it along the whole bore.
  • Bell: sound radiates through a circuit that approximates the radiation of an open pipe (Silva et al., 2009).
  • Breath noise: turbulent noise in the air leaking through the lips, switched on and off as the lips open and close (Chafe, 1990).

The parts are joined in the order the air travels: mouthpiece, tube with valves or slide, bell. Neighbouring parts exchange pressure and air flow at every step. The air comes in through the player's lips, which belong to the player's body and are described below. The simulation runs at 192 kHz and is brought down to 48 kHz for the renderer.

theREALpiano: a Steinway D

  • Strings: the length, diameter and tension of each string of a Steinway D, taken from Chabassier & Duruflé (2012). Each string vibrates in three directions, as a sum of its modes (Bank, Zambon & Fontana, 2010). Its tension rises as it swings wider (Ducceschi & Bilbao, 2022), and this produces the piano's phantom partials (Bank & Sujbert, 2005).
  • Hammers: felt that gets stiffer the harder it is squeezed, with each note's values from Chabassier & Duruflé (2012).
  • Action: key, wippen, jack, repetition lever and hammer as five rigid bodies touching through felt and leather, with friction, after Hirschkorn's (2004) measurements of a grand action. It is regulated in the simulation to the five distances a technician sets.
  • Dampers and pedal: each damper is a felt that the key lifts and that falls back under its own weight. The sustain pedal lifts all of them at once. Their parameters are my estimates.
  • Bridge and soundboard: strings and soundboard push on each other at the bridge in both directions (Chabassier, Chaigne & Joly, 2013). This is what gives a piano note its fast-then-slow decay (Weinreich, in Askenfelt, 1990). The soundboard has the outline, ribs, two bridges, woods and thicknesses of a Steinway D (Chabassier & Duruflé, 2012). Its lowest modes are in the right range but do not yet match Wogram's measurements (in Askenfelt, 1990) one by one.
  • Radiation: the soundboard's motion is turned into sound pressure at one metre, as a single point source.

The parts are joined in the order a note travels: key, action, hammer, strings, bridge, soundboard, sound. The hammer and the strings it strikes are solved together, and so are all the strings and the soundboard. Strings that are not played still ring along with the ones that are, whether or not the pedal is down. The simulation runs at 96 kHz. The pianist's fingertip pushes on the key, and the key pushes back.

The players

A player is modeled in two layers: a body that touches the instrument, and the way it senses, practices and decides what to do.

Bodies

Brass player.

  • Lungs: follow measured pressure–volume curves (Rahn et al., 1946) and the lung volumes of wind players (Bouhuys, 1964). As the air runs out, the lungs can no longer supply the pressure and the note sags.
  • Mouth and vocal tract: the mouth is an air volume. From the vocal folds to the lips is a 17 cm tube, computed the same way as the instrument's tube.
  • Tongue: a valve behind the teeth: tip for “t”, back for “k”. Releasing it starts the note (Hruška et al., 2015; Li et al., 2016).
  • Lips: two modes of vibration, one that swings outward and one that closes inward, with the parameters Cullen (2000) measured on artificial lips. Real players' lips show the same two modes (Newton, Campbell & Gilbert, 2008). Lip width and mass follow Boutin, Smith & Wolfe (2020). The player sets how tense they are, how far apart, and how hard to blow; blowing pressure rises with pitch (Fletcher & Tarnopolsky, 1999).
  • Fingers and arm: press the valves with a set force; the trombonist moves the slide from position to position.

The body is coupled to the instrument at the lips: the air the lips let through lowers the pressure in the mouth, and the pressure in the mouthpiece pushes back on the lips. Mouth, lips and instrument are solved together at every step.

Pianist.

  • Arms: shoulder, upper arm, forearm and hand as rigid bodies with mass, sized from anthropometric data (NASA, 1978). Joint stiffness follows Franklin & Milner (2003).
  • Hands and fingers: five fingers per hand, three joints each, with bone lengths from Buryanov & Kotiuk (2010). For black keys the hand moves further into the keyboard.
  • Muscles: a pair at each joint, one to bend and one to straighten. Their strength comes from the muscle cross-sections measured by Valero-Cuevas, Zajac & Burgar (1998).
  • Fingertip pads: soft, springy and slightly sticky, after the measurements of Wu et al. (2003). The pad is where the finger and the key push on each other.

What a player is allowed to know

To a player, the instrument is a black box. It can use only what a human player has: its ears, its sense of touch, the score, its intention, and what it remembered from earlier practice. The simulation knows much more, such as the exact air flow through the lips or the speed of each hammer. I use those values to check the physics, but they are kept out of what the player senses and out of what it is rewarded for. Where an experiment still breaks this rule, I note it.

Hearing and touch while playing

  • Ear: hears pitch differences as finely as a typical listener (Nelson, Stanton & Freyman, 1983). A human ear needs about 130 ms to react, so everything before that, like the lip setting at the start of a note, has to come from practice. After that, the ear corrects within the note.
  • Brass, in the note: the ear listens to the mouthpiece and nudges the lips toward the right pitch, only as far as real lips can bend a note (Boutin, Smith & Wolfe, 2020). If the note has slipped to a different harmonic, it is out of reach, and the player does not force it.
  • Brass, across the phrase: the player watches how much air is left and breathes where it planned. When the air runs short, the note sags physically. It does not yet decide on its own to shape the phrase around it.
  • Piano, touch: the pianist feels the key under the fingertip. It adds the weight of the arm only once the fingertip is on the key, relaxes once the key reaches the bottom, and in fast repeated notes waits until it feels the key come back up.
  • Piano, ear: listens to each note it plays. On the next note it plays a little harder if this one came out weak, or a little earlier if it came out late.

Practice tables

Before it performs, a player practices on its instrument and writes down what it learned. Each entry is indexed by an action, not by a piece, so what it learns on one piece carries over to the next. Each table is also tagged with the exact version of the instrument and the player it was learned on. When the physics changes, the old entries are not reused.

  • Brass, lip map: for every fingering and blowing pressure, the player sweeps its lip tension and records where each note speaks and where it sits in tune. A setting counts as learned only when it is in tune within what its ear can hear.
  • Brass, breath: before playing a phrase for the first time, the player rehearses it once and records how much air each note takes. It then picks where to breathe so that every breath lasts, with as few breaths as possible and none in the middle of a slur. Rahn et al. (1946) give how the lungs behave; when to inhale follows Cugell (1986).
  • Piano, touch table: for each finger, key, starting height and force, the pianist records how fast the hammer flies and how long the key takes to reach the string.
  • Piano, change table: for each move from one finger and key to the next, the pianist records how early to start and how much to adjust the force. A new passage is rehearsed before it is played.

Reinforcement learning

Practice tables correct one quantity at a time, toward a target. Some problems have no single target. A note can be in tune and still start dirty, lock in slowly or come out too soft. For these I am testing reinforcement learning. The player tries variations of one or two actions that a human would actually control, and keeps the variations that a reward prefers. The reward lowers the score when a note fails to sound, lands on the wrong harmonic, is late, too soft or cut short.

  • Brass, from one note to the next: for each kind of note change, the player learns how to set its lips just before the new note and how quickly to release the tongue or slide the lips. The reward comes only from what its ear hears in the first moments of the new note.
  • Piano, fast repeated notes: the pianist learns when to let go after the key gives way, how high to wait above the bottom, and how far the key must come back before pressing again. This reward still uses the hammer's speed and timing from the simulation, which a pianist cannot sense. It is the one place where the rule above is still broken.

Both use a simple search over a few parameters (the cross-entropy method), not a deep neural network. Systems that learn from sound and adjust only a few parameters, such as Percival, Bailey & Tzanetakis's (2011) virtual violinist, do the same. Deep-learning piano and drum robots need millions of trials and one network per piece, and they read key positions no human can sense (Zakka et al., 2023). Each learned version is tested three ways, as in Continual World (Wołczyk et al., 2021). Does the practiced phrase improve? Does the skill carry over to phrases it has not practiced? Does it spoil skills the player already had? So far the practiced phrases improved, but on some unpracticed phrases the learned habit made things worse. The learned versions are therefore not yet used in the default renders.

Open problems

  • Horn and tuba do not yet play convincingly. Their bore shapes are inferred from sparse measurements, and I am writing practice material suited to each instrument instead of transposing the trumpet's.
  • Piano chords: when three fingers flex together, the reaction lifts the hand and outer fingers leave their keys. Rehearsal cannot fix this; the hand mechanics have to change.
  • Piano dynamic range is narrower than a real instrument's, and the radiation model is still a simple monopole.
  • Evaluation: so far the instruments have been checked against published measurements and by my own listening. I have not yet tested them with other composers and performers, or measured how much the player model contributes to what listeners hear.

Next

  • Piano: rebuilding the pianist on the same practice model as the brass player, so it learns its own touch and fingering.
  • Brass multiphonics by singing while playing, through a vocal tract coupled to the player's mouth, and lip parameters suited to low brass.
  • The rest of the practice course: common melodies, multiphonics inside a melody, and playing without a mouthpiece.
  • Upright bass and drum kit, toward a jazz quartet of virtual players.
  • Speculative instruments assembled from modeled parts, and an interface for composers to try a technique and hear how a player gets there.

How I work

I set the direction, listen to every render, and make the musical and physical judgments. My ear is the final criterion; measurements locate causes and catch destructive errors. Each listening round asks one question, with the same score, seed and gain. Every design decision is documented next to the code with its sources, and the findings that hold up will become a paper. The instruments render through an audio renderer I built for this project.
