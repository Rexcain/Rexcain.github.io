Research

theREALinst

Physically modeled instruments, played by virtual performers who have to learn them first.

In development since 2026 · C++, Apple Metal, Python · Dartmouth College

How does a virtual instrument change when it comes with a player who must learn to control it?

Most physical models are played by writing their control signals directly: a lip frequency curve for a trumpet, a hammer velocity for a piano note. Real instruments are not played that way. A trumpeter does not know the lip frequency of a G5; they know what it feels like to land it, and they learned that by practicing on one instrument with one body.

theREALinst pairs each instrument model with a simulated performer. The performer practices on that particular instrument, keeps what it learned, and then plays a score by listening and correcting itself. The same score on a different instrument, or with a different player, comes out differently for physical reasons rather than random ones. My longer aim is a tool that lets composers and performers explore techniques and instruments they cannot easily get their hands on, including speculative instruments assembled from modeled parts.

The project began as the audio engine of a game I am co-developing and has grown into the center of my research.

The instruments

theREALbrass: trumpet, trombone, horn, tuba

The air column is simulated sample by sample, following the finite-difference approach of the NESS project (Bilbao & Chick, 2013); the trumpet's resonances land within 1% of their published measurements. The lips vibrate in two modes, after Cullen's (2000) measurements on artificial lips, so they can sound on either side of a resonance as real players do. The valves have mass, springs and felt, can be half-pressed, and click when pressed. At loud dynamics the sound turns brassy through nonlinear wave steepening (Myers et al., 2012).

theREALpiano: a Steinway D

Built from the published string, hammer and bridge parameters of a Steinway D (Chabassier & Duruflé, 2012). The strings vibrate in three directions with geometric nonlinearity, which gives rise to phantom partials (Chabassier, Chaigne & Joly, 2013; Ducceschi & Bilbao, 2022). The soundboard has ribs and two bridges, and the grand action, with escapement, dampers and pedal, follows Hirschkorn's (2004) model.

The players

A brass player that practices like a student

It practices the way a student does: long tones, scales, tonguing, arpeggios, études. It finds its own fingerings, pulls a slide when a note sits sharp, switches to double tonguing when the notes get fast, and decides where to breathe. Its ear tells pitches apart as finely as a typical listener (Nelson, Stanton & Freyman, 1983), and its lungs follow measured pressure–volume data (Rahn et al., 1946).

A pianist with a body

Its arms, hands and fingers are built from anthropometric data (NASA, 1978), with finger muscles after Valero-Cuevas et al. (1998) and fingertip pads after Wu et al. (2003). It calibrates its touch finger by finger and rehearses before it plays. For its loudest notes it strikes the key from above, as pianists do (Goebl et al., 2005; Furuya et al., 2010).

Open problems

  • Horn and tuba do not yet play convincingly. Their bore shapes are inferred from sparse measurements, and I am writing practice material suited to each instrument instead of transposing the trumpet's.
  • Piano chords: when three fingers flex together, the reaction lifts the hand and outer fingers leave their keys. Rehearsal cannot fix this; the hand mechanics have to change.
  • Piano dynamic range is narrower than a real instrument's, and the radiation model is still a simple monopole.
  • Evaluation: so far the instruments have been checked against published measurements and by my own listening. I have not yet tested them with other composers and performers, or measured how much the player model contributes to what listeners hear.

Next

  • Piano: rebuilding the pianist on the same practice model as the brass player, so it learns its own touch and fingering. Audio coming soon.
  • Brass multiphonics by singing while playing, through a vocal tract coupled to the player's mouth, and lip parameters suited to low brass.
  • The rest of the practice course: common melodies, multiphonics inside a melody, and playing without a mouthpiece.
  • Upright bass and drum kit, toward a jazz quartet of virtual players.
  • Speculative instruments assembled from modeled parts, and an interface for composers to try a technique and hear how a player gets there.

How I work

I set the direction, listen to every render, and make the musical and physical judgments. Much of the implementation is written with Claude Code, and every design decision is documented next to the code with its sources. The instruments render through a headless offline DAW I built for this project.
